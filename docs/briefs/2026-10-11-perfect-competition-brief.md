# Perfect Competition — Engagement Brief

## The problem

The farm must decide how to allocate its 64 available growing beds among tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun to maximize seasonal profit. The market determines the revenue per bed for each crop, so the farmer cannot choose prices and must make the best production decision given those prices. The farmer must also account for fertilizer expenses, limited labor availability, labor costs, fixed seasonal costs, and the maximum number of beds that can be devoted to each crop. The challenge is to choose a crop mix that generates the greatest profit rather than simply the most revenue.

## What I am assuming

I assume the prices, fertilizer costs, growing-season length, labor availability, and crop-specific bed limits provided in the case are accurate and remain fixed for the season. I also assume the farm should consider both its own limited labor and the cost of hiring temporary workers when deciding how many beds to plant. Because labor requirements increase as more beds of a crop are planted, I expect the relative attractiveness of each crop to change as production expands. I am not assuming that the crop with the highest revenue per bed will necessarily generate the greatest profit.

## Hypothesis

I predict that the most profitable allocation will be 16 tomato beds, 18 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. I expect the farm to plant a substantial number of tomato beds because tomatoes generate considerably more revenue per bed than the other crops. However, I predict that the farm will stop short of the maximum 20 beds because tomatoes have the highest diminishing-returns rate, at 10%, which may cause the additional labor required for further production to make those beds less attractive. I predict that mesclun will use all 30 available beds because it generates more revenue per bed than carrots and has the lowest diminishing-returns rate, at 1.25%. Although mesclun has higher fertilizer costs than carrots, its higher revenue and slower increase in labor requirements may make it more attractive as production expands. I expect carrots to occupy the remaining 18 beds because their lower initial labor requirements and fertilizer costs make them a reasonable alternative to expanding tomato or mesclun production further. The specific allocation is my initial prediction, and I expect the model to test whether it actually maximizes profit.

## How I would know I was wrong

My hypothesis would be wrong if the optimization model found that a different feasible allocation of the 64 beds produced a higher seasonal profit than 16 tomato beds, 18 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. This would show that my predicted crop mix does not maximize profit under the case's assumptions. I would then examine the model's results to understand how labor requirements, fertilizer costs, and the diminishing-returns rates affected the relative profitability of the crops.
